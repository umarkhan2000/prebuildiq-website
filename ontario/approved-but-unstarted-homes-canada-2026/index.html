143,929 Approved-But-Unstarted Homes in Canada: What the September 2026 CMHC Data Shows

Published October 2026· Updated October 2026· 7 min read· PreBuildIQ Research Team
Quick Answer

CMHC data for September 2026 shows 143,929 homes across Canada have an approved building permit with no construction started, up 1.1% month-to-month. The total annual pace of housing starts for September was 248,896 units seasonally adjusted, up 3% from August, but actual starts in centres of 10,000 or more were down 11% year-over-year at 19,984 units compared with 22,375 in September 2025. Statistics Canada reported that Canada issued $13.6 billion in building permits in August 2026, up 8.8% from July. The gap between permits issued and homes started reflects the time required to convert an approved permit into a shovel-ready project: complete financing, contractor procurement, site preparation, and pre-construction work. In Ontario, where the GTA approval window runs 18 to 24 months from first submission to building permit, a project that cleared that entitlement process can still sit for months before breaking ground. The policy response in 2026, which has focused heavily on development charge reductions, addresses cost. It does not address the entitlement-to-shovel delay, which is the dominant reason the backlog exists.

Canada issued $13.6 billion in building permits in August 2026. In September, 143,929 of those permitted homes had no shovel in the ground. The number grew 1.1% month-to-month, and actual housing starts, the count of homes where construction has physically begun, fell 11% year-over-year in September, at 19,984 units versus 22,375 in September 2025.

Two statistics in the same month: more permits, fewer starts. The gap between them is the story of Canadian housing supply in 2026, and it is not a demand story or a fee story. It is an entitlement-to-shovel story.

Why the Backlog Exists

An approved building permit is a legal authorization to build. It is not a funding commitment, a construction contract, or a site ready for a crane. Between permit and shovel, a project typically needs:

  • Construction financing. Many permits are pulled to lock in approvals or DC rates, before the construction loan is fully committed. Rising interest rates and lender caution in 2026 have extended the financing close timeline.
  • Contractor procurement. In a tight labour market, even a fully funded project can wait months for a general contractor with available capacity.
  • Preconstruction work. Demolition, dewatering, shoring, and site servicing all happen before the first foundation pour counts as a housing start. These can take 3 to 6 months on a complex urban site.
  • Staged build programs. Some large master-planned communities pull permits for phases that are 12 to 24 months away from starting, to lock in current DC rates before a scheduled increase.
The 2026 DC reductions solve for cost. They do not solve for time.

Toronto and Mississauga have cut development charges materially in 2026. A developer who captures that saving still faces the same entitlement and preconstruction timeline before breaking ground. Fee relief and approval speed are different levers.

What It Means for Ontario Developers

For a developer running a pro forma in 2026, the backlog data has two practical implications:

  • The window for DC savings is timing-dependent. Toronto's reduction program runs to 2029, Mississauga's to March 2029. A project already permitted can start construction now to lock in the saving. A project still in entitlements is working against the clock on both the approval timeline and the DC window.
  • Actual starts are the lagging indicator. Permit value rising while starts fall means the supply that the policy environment has been trying to stimulate is still sitting in the gap. Developers whose projects are entitlement-complete are in the best position to convert that gap into starts.

The CMHC data also signals something about lender sentiment: the projects not starting are not doing so because there is no demand or no permission. They are not starting because the numbers do not yet work, or the money is not yet closed. In 2026, the dominant constraint on Ontario housing supply is not the permit. It is everything that happens between permit and pour.

Example
A developer with four entitled sites in Mississauga had permits in hand by mid-2026, capturing the city's 50 percent DC reduction on all four projects. Two broke ground immediately. Two sat through Q3 2026 while the construction loan was finalized and a GC was procured. Those two show up in the approved-but-unstarted count. The permit was not the bottleneck. Financing and procurement were.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many approved-but-unstarted homes are in Canada in 2026?

CMHC data for September 2026 shows 143,929 homes in Canada have an approved building permit with no construction started, up 1.1% month-to-month from August 2026.

Why are housing starts falling if building permits are rising?

Building permits and housing starts measure different things. A permit authorizes construction; a start is counted when construction physically begins. The gap between them reflects the time needed to close construction financing, procure contractors, complete preconstruction site work, and stage phased developments. In September 2026, actual housing starts in centres of 10,000 or more were down 11% year-over-year while permit value rose, showing the conversion from permit to start is taking longer than in prior years.

What is the approved-but-unstarted backlog in Canada?

The approved-but-unstarted count is the number of housing units that have received a building permit from a Canadian municipality but have not yet had construction begin. CMHC tracks this as a leading indicator of future supply. As of September 2026 the count was 143,929 units and growing, indicating a large pipeline of authorized supply that has not yet converted to physical homes.

Do development charge cuts fix the approved-but-unstarted problem?

No. Development charge reductions in 2026, including Toronto's 40 to 60 percent cut and Mississauga's 50 percent cut, address project cost. The approved-but-unstarted backlog is driven by financing timelines, contractor availability, and preconstruction work, not by the cost of the permit itself. Fee relief and approval speed are different levers that address different parts of the supply problem.

What is the SAAR for Canadian housing starts in September 2026?

The seasonally adjusted annualized rate of housing starts for Canada in September 2026 was 248,896 units, up 3% from 242,223 in August 2026. The six-month trend was 250,411 units. Actual starts in centres of 10,000 or more were 19,984 units, down 11% from 22,375 in September 2025.
