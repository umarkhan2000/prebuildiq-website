What Is an Urban Design Brief in Ontario?

Published August 2026· Updated August 2026· 7 min read· PreBuildIQ Research Team
Quick Answer

An Urban Design Brief sets out the design rationale for a development, how the built form, massing, streetscape, and public realm respond to their context and to municipal urban design guidelines. It is required for most mid- and high-rise, mixed-use, and intensification applications and for many site plan submissions. It is prepared by an architect or urban designer and becomes the reference the municipality uses to judge whether later detailed drawings deliver the promised design quality.

The Urban Design Brief is the document that turns a massing model into a commitment. It is where a developer explains why the building looks the way it does, and municipalities increasingly use it as the yardstick for every later drawing. A weak brief invites open-ended design negotiation; a strong one narrows what staff can ask for.

What Is a Urban Design Brief?

An Urban Design Brief is an illustrated report that explains and justifies the design of a proposed development. It covers the relationship to surrounding context, building massing and height transitions, the treatment of the street and public realm, pedestrian circulation, and how the design meets the municipality’s urban design guidelines and official plan design policies.

Unlike an engineering study, the brief is partly a persuasive document. It sets the design intent that the site plan drawings, elevations, and landscape plans must then honour. On tall-building applications it typically addresses shadow, wind, and skyview considerations at a conceptual level, cross-referencing the dedicated studies where they exist.

When Is It Required?

An Urban Design Brief is required for design-sensitive applications, including:

  • Mid-rise, high-rise, and tall-building proposals
  • Mixed-use and intensification applications
  • Development in a designated urban growth or transit-oriented area
  • Site plan applications in areas with urban design guidelines
  • Proposals seeking height or density beyond the as-of-right form

Who Prepares It

An Urban Design Brief is prepared by an architect or urban designer, often the project architect or a dedicated urban design consultant. Municipal urban design staff, and in some cities a design review panel, evaluate the brief and can require revisions before the application advances.

What It Must Contain

A complete Urban Design Brief typically contains:

  • A context and site analysis
  • The design vision, massing, and height transition strategy
  • Streetscape, public realm, and pedestrian experience
  • Building articulation, materials, and ground-floor treatment
  • Response to municipal urban design guidelines and official plan policy
  • Conceptual shadow, wind, and skyview considerations

Budget note

On tall buildings the brief often runs alongside a design review panel process that can require one or more resubmissions. Each panel cycle adds weeks, so budget for iteration, not a single review.

How Requirements Vary Across Ontario Municipalities

Cities with mature design cultures, Toronto, Mississauga, Vaughan, Markham, apply detailed urban design guidelines and, for tall buildings, formal design review panels, which raise both the depth of the brief and the number of review cycles. Smaller municipalities may require a lighter brief tied to a specific site plan. Growth areas and Major Transit Station Areas often carry their own design guidelines that the brief must specifically address.

Example
A tall-building application submitted a concise design brief that met the general official plan policy but not the area-specific tall-building guidelines. The design review panel requested a stepped podium, revised shadow response, and a wider setback at grade. Two panel cycles and the redesign added roughly three months before the site plan could proceed.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is an urban design brief?

An urban design brief is an illustrated report that explains and justifies a development’s design, its context response, massing, streetscape, and public realm, and shows how it meets municipal urban design guidelines and official plan design policy. It is prepared by an architect or urban designer and becomes the reference for later detailed drawings.

When is an urban design brief required in Ontario?

An urban design brief is required for most mid- and high-rise, mixed-use, and intensification applications, for site plan submissions in areas with urban design guidelines, and for proposals seeking height or density beyond the as-of-right form. Growth areas and transit-oriented sites almost always require one.

How much does an urban design brief cost?

An urban design brief typically costs between $8,000 and $35,000 or more, driven by the building height, the scope of renderings, and whether a formal design review panel is involved. Tall-building panels often require resubmissions, so the budget should account for iteration.

Who prepares an urban design brief?

An urban design brief is prepared by an architect or urban designer, frequently the project architect or a dedicated urban design consultant. Municipal urban design staff, and in some cities a design review panel, review it and can require revisions before the application proceeds.

What is the difference between an urban design brief and a planning justification report?

The urban design brief makes the case for how the building looks and meets its site, massing, materials, and public realm. The planning justification report makes the case for why the use and density are good planning under policy. They are complementary: one is a design argument, the other a policy argument, and major applications need both.
