What Is an Archaeological Assessment in Ontario?

Published August 2026· Updated August 2026· 7 min read· PreBuildIQ Research Team
Quick Answer

An Archaeological Assessment determines whether a site contains archaeological resources that must be protected or documented before development disturbs the ground. It is required where a site has archaeological potential, near water, on early settlement routes, or flagged by provincial screening. It is conducted in up to four stages by a provincially licensed archaeologist, and a positive finding can require excavation or avoidance that delays and reshapes a project.

Archaeology is a study governed by what might be buried, not by what is visible. Proximity to water, historic transportation routes, or known sites gives land archaeological potential, and once flagged, the ground cannot be disturbed until a licensed archaeologist has cleared it. A late-discovered Stage 2 requirement can stall site works entirely.

What Is a Archaeological Assessment?

An Archaeological Assessment is a staged investigation, conducted under the Ontario Heritage Act, to identify and protect archaeological resources. Stage 1 is a background and potential study; Stage 2 is a field survey; Stages 3 and 4 involve site-specific assessment and mitigation, excavation or protection, only if resources are found.

The work must be carried out by an archaeologist licensed by the Ministry of Citizenship and Multiculturalism, and the resulting report is submitted to the ministry, which enters it into the provincial registry. A planning application on land with archaeological potential is not deemed complete until at least the initial assessment clears the site or identifies what must be protected.

When Is It Required?

An Archaeological Assessment is triggered where a site has archaeological potential, including:

  • Land within a set distance of a watercourse, shoreline, or wetland
  • Sites on or near historic settlement, trail, or transportation routes
  • Proximity to registered or known archaeological sites
  • Areas flagged by a municipal archaeological potential map
  • Subdivision, rezoning, or servicing on previously undisturbed land

Who Prepares It

An Archaeological Assessment is conducted by an archaeologist licensed under the Ontario Heritage Act by the Ministry of Citizenship and Multiculturalism. The archaeologist submits the assessment report to the ministry for review and registration, and the municipality relies on that clearance before allowing ground disturbance.

What It Must Contain

A complete Archaeological Assessment program typically involves:

  • Stage 1: background research and assessment of archaeological potential
  • Stage 2: property field survey where potential exists
  • Stage 3: site-specific assessment of any resources found
  • Stage 4: mitigation through excavation or protection
  • Reporting to the province and entry in the archaeological registry
  • Any First Nations engagement the ministry or context requires

Budget note

Cost is bimodal. Many sites clear at Stage 1 or 2 for a few thousand dollars, but a site that reaches Stage 3 or 4 excavation can cost tens of thousands and add months. Because Stage 2 needs frost-free ground, a winter start can idle a project until spring.

How Requirements Vary Across Ontario Municipalities

Most Ontario municipalities maintain or reference an archaeological potential model, and many require a Stage 1 assessment as a standard condition for subdivisions and rezonings on undisturbed land. Municipalities near the Great Lakes, major rivers, and historic settlement corridors flag more land as high potential. Where a site is near an Indigenous community or known burial area, the province may require additional engagement that extends the process.

Example
A greenfield subdivision near a river assumed the land was clear and scheduled grading for early spring. The Stage 1 assessment flagged archaeological potential along the watercourse, and the Stage 2 field survey could not proceed until the ground thawed. The sequencing pushed servicing works back a full season.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is an archaeological assessment?

An archaeological assessment is a staged investigation under the Ontario Heritage Act to identify and protect archaeological resources before development disturbs the ground. It runs in up to four stages, from a background potential study to field survey and, if resources are found, excavation or protection. It is conducted by a provincially licensed archaeologist.

When is an archaeological assessment required in Ontario?

An archaeological assessment is required where a site has archaeological potential: near water, on or near historic settlement and transportation routes, close to known archaeological sites, or flagged by a municipal potential map. Subdivisions, rezonings, and servicing on previously undisturbed land commonly require at least a Stage 1 assessment.

How much does an archaeological assessment cost?

Cost varies widely. Many sites clear at Stage 1 or 2 for a few thousand dollars, but a site that advances to Stage 3 or 4 excavation can cost tens of thousands of dollars and add months. The Stage 2 field survey also needs frost-free ground, so timing affects the schedule.

What are the stages of an archaeological assessment?

Stage 1 is background research assessing archaeological potential. Stage 2 is a property field survey where potential exists. Stage 3 is a site-specific assessment of any resources found. Stage 4 is mitigation, either excavation or protection. Most sites stop after Stage 1 or 2; only sites with confirmed resources reach Stages 3 and 4.

Who can perform an archaeological assessment in Ontario?

Only an archaeologist licensed under the Ontario Heritage Act by the Ministry of Citizenship and Multiculturalism can conduct an archaeological assessment. The licensed archaeologist submits the report to the province, which reviews it and enters it into the archaeological sites registry before ground disturbance is allowed.
